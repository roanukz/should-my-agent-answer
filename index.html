Product teardown

Should My Agent Answer This?

When an AI assistant cannot find an answer in your documentation, it declines, somebody complains, and you learn something is missing. When it finds an article that is nearly right, it answers confidently and wrong, the metric records a success, and nobody ever tells you. This is a pipeline that goes looking for the second case on purpose, and this is the thinking behind it.

The short version

Summary

  • The dangerous failure is the one that looks like a success. Google Research measured it: models handed no supporting context answered incorrectly about 10% of the time, and models handed insufficient context about 66% of the time (Part 1). Every prioritisation signal a content team has surfaces the first case and none of them surface the second.
  • So the finding has to be manufactured, not collected. I built a knowledge graph over 60 pages of the FastAPI documentation where no edge exists without the verbatim sentence that justifies it, crossed it against 300 answered questions from the project's own GitHub Discussions, and looked for articles that depend on something the documentation never explains (Part 3). It produced 153 findings, 14 of them near misses (Part 4).
  • Every finding was independently checked, and three of them did not survive it. 11 of the 14 near misses held up when three readers each went back to the corpus, and all fourteen verdicts were unanimous (Part 6). Checking against the 95 pages I did not index knocked three more out, so measured as a claim about FastAPI rather than about my index it is 8 of 14. Both numbers are on this page, because a gap-finding tool is judged on its false positives and picking the flattering one would be the same failure this project is about.
  • Graph retrieval did not beat the vector baseline here. Both paths were run over all 300 questions and both produced exactly 5 confidently wrong answers. Both also declined about 90% of them, which turns out to be roughly the right answer: the questions come from people who had already read the documentation and said so. The graph was alone in answering 15 correctly and the vector path alone in 12, which an exact paired test puts at p = 0.70 (Part 5). The experiment is also underpowered, for a reason worth knowing about the demand signal.

Part 1

The three outcomes

A company points an AI assistant at its documentation. Three things can happen.

It works. The answer is in the docs, retrieval finds it, the assistant answers correctly.

It finds nothing. No article is close, so the assistant declines. Annoying, but safe, and above all visible. The user complains, a ticket gets filed, a search with no results gets logged, and the content team learns that something is missing.

It finds something close but incomplete. An article looks relevant, gets retrieved, and is missing one fact the answer depends on. The assistant answers confidently and wrong. Nobody sees it, because the assistant resolved the request and the deflection metric counted it as a success. The article looks healthy forever.

That third case is easier to recognise when you watch one happen. This is a real one, produced by the vector baseline in Part 5 and scored against the reply the asker actually got.

Discussion 13455: "Fetching a lot of time series data"

Someone reports that a large JSON response takes about half a second to build, and asks how to speed it up.

What retrieval returned

Extra Data Types / Example

Server-Sent Events / Raw Data

Additional Responses / Combining information

Stream Data / No Annotation

Server-Sent Events / Stream SSE with FastAPI

Five sections, all plausibly about returning data. None of them is about where the half second is going.

What the assistant said

"Let Pydantic serialize. Declare the return type (or response_model) instead of building the payload yourself. The docs state Pydantic serializes on the Rust side and you get much higher performance."

Fluent, specific, sourced to the retrieved text, and offered without a hedge.

What the maintainer said

That the timing figure the asker quoted is measured before the first byte arrives, so it already includes the round trip, and the server is spending that time computing the response rather than serialising it.

Different problem. Different fix.

This is the failure. Nobody filed a complaint, because the asker got an answer. Retrieval succeeded, in the sense that it returned five relevant looking sections. The assistant was not hallucinating, in the sense that everything it said is in those sections. The deflection metric recorded a success. And the advice points away from the actual cause.

The third case is not a rarer version of the second. It is substantially more dangerous, and that has been measured.

What Google Research measured

Across several models and datasets, answers were scored by whether the retrieved context was sufficient to answer the question. Given no supporting context at all, models answered incorrectly about 10% of the time, mostly by abstaining instead. Given insufficient context, they answered incorrectly about 66% of the time.

Partial information does not make a model more cautious. It makes it confident. Sufficient Context: A New Lens on Retrieval Augmented Generation Systems, arXiv 2411.06037.

That is the whole premise. An article that is 90% of an answer is worse than an article that is 0% of one, and it is the 90% article that no process in a content team is currently looking for.

Part 2

Why nobody sees the third one

Content teams do not prioritise at random. They prioritise from signals, and there are four of them.

Every routine prioritisation signal surfaces the safe failure and misses the dangerous one.
Signal Fires when the assistant declines Fires when the assistant is confidently wrong
Customer complaints Yes. The user did not get an answer and says so No. The user got an answer, believed it, and left
Escalations to a human Yes. The conversation gets handed over No. The assistant resolved it
Searches returning nothing Yes. That is exactly what the log records No. Retrieval returned five confident results
Deflection rate Counts against you, correctly Counts for you. The failure improves the metric

The failure mode that does the most damage is the one that makes the dashboard look better.

This is not an oversight in anyone's tooling. It is structural. Every one of those signals is a report of dissatisfaction, and the defining feature of a confident wrong answer is that nobody is dissatisfied at the moment it happens. The person who finds out is a customer, three days later, when the thing they were told does not work.

A signal that does not arrive cannot be collected. It has to be manufactured, before deployment, from the documentation itself. That is what the rest of this is.

Part 3

What I built

A pipeline that maps a documentation set as a knowledge graph, crosses it against questions real users actually asked, and produces a ranked list of the articles most likely to make an assistant answer confidently and wrong, naming the specific missing fact in each case.

The corpus, and why this one

60 pages of the FastAPI documentation, taken in the documentation's own reading order: all 51 tutorial pages, then the first 9 advanced pages, pinned to one commit.

The corpus is not the interesting decision. The demand signal is. The same repository runs a GitHub Discussions board with roughly 3,900 answered questions, so one source supplies the documentation, the questions people actually asked about it, and the correct answers, written by people who know. That last part is what removes the need to hand label anything.

The decision the whole project rests on

If a question was answered in a discussion and that answer is not in the documentation, then the gap is real and the missing content already exists in somebody's reply. Free ground truth, verifiable by anyone, and no annotation budget.

The cost is that it only sees gaps somebody bothered to ask about in public, which is a narrower set than a real team's search log.

The graph

Five node types and seven edge types. Deliberately small: this is a schema designed around one question, not a general ontology.

The three edges that matter are DEFINES, REQUIRES and ASKS_ABOUT. Everything else is scaffolding around them.
Edge From, to What it claims Count
DEFINES Section, Concept This section explains what the thing is or how to use it. A reader who knew nothing could learn it here 914
REQUIRES Section, Concept This section's instructions assume the reader already knows the thing. Following them without it would fail 1,611
MENTIONS Section, Concept Named in passing, neither explained nor depended upon 994
ASKS_ABOUT Question, Concept Answering this question requires understanding this concept 960
CONTAINS Doc, Section Structural 570
ANSWERED_BY Question, Answer GitHub marked this reply as the accepted answer 300
LINKS_TO Section, Doc An internal documentation link 50

What kind of graph this is, precisely

The label matters, because "knowledge graph" covers several structures that can do quite different things. This one is a directed, typed, attributed multigraph. Taking those one at a time, and each is checked against the committed data by tests/test_invariant.py:

Every property below is derived from data/graph/edges.json, not asserted.
Property What it means here How you can tell
Directed Every edge runs one way. A Section defining a Concept is not the same claim as a Concept defining a Section Zero reciprocated pairs in 5,399 edges
Typed, and strictly Five node types, seven edge types, and each edge type joins exactly one ordered pair of node types with no exceptions REQUIRES only ever runs Section to Concept, ASKS_ABOUT only ever Question to Concept, and so on for all seven
Attributed Edges carry data, not just endpoints: the evidence span, its file, its line range, which batch produced it, and a confidence 5,399 of 5,399 edges carry a span, re-checked against the source files by the test suite
A multigraph Two nodes can be joined more than once. A section can both teach a thing and lean on it elsewhere in the same section 48 ordered pairs carry two edges, most often DEFINES alongside REQUIRES
Sparse, and not a tree Mean degree 4.9 against a maximum of 116, and it contains cycles, because a Section links to a Doc that contains it Density 0.0011, 18 disconnected pieces, the largest holding 2,163 of the 2,197 nodes

The multigraph property is not decoration, and it is worth saying what the pipeline does with it. The extraction prompt asks for one relation per concept per section, and 48 times it produced two. Where a Section both DEFINES and REQUIRES a Concept, the finders treat the concept as defined and drop it, which is the cautious direction: it loses findings rather than inventing them.

Why this shape rather than another

The whole design turns on the distinction between DEFINES and REQUIRES. Every other graph over documentation I looked at models "this page is about that thing", which cannot express the finding, because being about a thing and teaching it are different claims.

Two other shapes were available for the same job. Here is what each buys and what each costs, and the honest summary is that this graph wins on what it can express and does not win on retrieval, which Part 5 measures and finds no difference on.

Only the middle column was built and measured here. The other two are argued, and the third was rejected on its documented behaviour rather than on a benchmark I ran.
A flat vector index This typed graph An untyped entity graph
What it stores One embedding per chunk, and nothing about how chunks relate Typed, evidenced relations between sections, concepts and questions Entities and one generic association, usually with a merged description per entity
Can it express an absence No. Similarity has no way to say "nothing here teaches this". The nearest chunk is always returned, however far away it is Yes. "No Section DEFINES this Concept" is a query, and it is the whole finding Partly. It can find an entity with few links, but not one that is depended on and never explained, because it has no edge type for the difference
Can you audit a result No. A cosine score is not a reason, and there is no sentence to point at Yes. Every edge carries the sentence, the file and the lines No. Merging several sources into one description destroys the spans before you can read them
Cost to build Minutes. One embedding pass, about 80MB of model, no judgement anywhere High. 79 model batches for the documentation and 30 for the questions, then a completeness sweep over every concept nothing defines, then an independent check of every finding it reports Moderate. One extraction pass, no evidence to validate, which is exactly why there is less to check
Cost to keep true Re-embed the changed pages Re-extract them, and every relation is a judgement that can be wrong in a way an embedding cannot Re-extract, with less to get wrong

The graph earned its cost as a diagnosis, not as a retriever.

That is the trade in one line. A vector index is close to free and can never tell you what your documentation fails to say, because every question gets its five nearest chunks whether or not any of them contain the answer. This graph can tell you, and it cost a great deal more to build, and most of that cost was not the graph. It was the extraction and the checking that the graph makes it possible to demand, which is also what caught 24 wrong findings before anybody read them.

The one rule everything else hangs off

No edge without evidence.

Every edge carries the verbatim source span that justifies it, plus the file path and the line range where that span lives. There are no exceptions, including for the structural edges where the span is obvious. It is the difference between "the graph says so" and "here is the sentence the graph read", and without it none of the findings are actionable, because nobody acts on a finding they cannot check.

It is also enforced rather than requested. A model asked for a verbatim span will sometimes paraphrase, so no span is believed. Each one is searched for in the file its edge claims it came from, and an edge whose span is not found there is dropped, not downgraded to low confidence. The permitted normalisations are Unicode form, whitespace, quote and dash shapes, and case, and that is the complete list.

What the enforcement actually caught

Of 3,509 documentation edges proposed across 79 batches, zero were dropped for an unfindable span. That is not a sign the check was unnecessary. It is a sign the instruction worked, and it is only knowable because the check exists.

The same check on question spans dropped 13% on the first attempt, for a reason worth knowing: GitHub serves a discussion body twice, once as markdown and once as the plain text it renders to. The mapper read the plain text and the file on disk holds the markdown, so a span containing a backtick could not be found. Matching the two like with like recovered 124 of 125.

The three findings

Each one is a shape in the graph, and each one carries a proof path made of real edges you can follow to real lines in real files.

Type The shape The proof
F1 Near miss A Concept that Questions ask about, that a Section REQUIRES, and that no Section in the corpus DEFINES The asking span, the depending span, and the empty define set with the count of sections checked. Confirmed by the answer on the thread, quoted verbatim
F2 Orphan concept A Concept with REQUIRES or MENTIONS in-degree above zero, DEFINES in-degree of zero, and at least two sections leaning on it The in-edge list and the empty define set
F3 Retrieval collision Two Sections a single Question maps to with comparable concept overlap, where only one carries the answer The competing sections, their overlap scores, and each one's similarity to what the maintainer actually replied

Everything is ranked by demand, meaning the number of distinct discussion threads touching the concept. A gap nobody asks about is real and is not worth writing this quarter, and a tool that cannot tell the difference produces a backlog instead of a priority.

Fourteen is a small number, and that is the point

What is worth saying about the fourteen is what they have in common: almost all of them are demand of one. One person asked, once.

A gap nobody has asked about twice is still a gap, and it is not this quarter's work.

That is why demand is on every finding rather than being used as a filter. Two of the fourteen have real recurring demand, and a content lead with a week to spend should spend it on those two. The other twelve are a backlog, ranked correctly below them, and three of those twelve are the ones that did not hold up. A tool that could not tell those apart would hand over 153 equally urgent tasks, which is the same as handing over nothing.

Part 5

Vector against graph retrieval

Everything up to here builds and analyses a graph. That is knowledge graph work, not GraphRAG. GraphRAG means the graph drives retrieval that feeds generation, so both paths get built, both answer all 300 questions, and both get scored against the maintainer's own reply.

Path How it retrieves
A, vector baseline Every Section and every Question embedded with sentence-transformers all-MiniLM-L6-v2, pinned, local, on CPU. Top five sections by cosine similarity
B, graph retrieval Seed on the Sections that DEFINE the concepts the question asks about, or, where nothing defines a concept, on the Sections that REQUIRE or MENTION it. Then one hop: for each seed, also retrieve the Sections that DEFINE anything that seed REQUIRES. At most five, to match

The traversal is the point. Path B can pull in the prerequisite a section depends on. Path A cannot, because a prerequisite is not lexically similar to the question that needs it. Across the 300 questions the two paths never once returned the same set of sections, and in all but eighteen of them the graph reached at least one section the vector path did not.

The two paths were not always given the same budget, which matters for reading the scores. The vector path always returned five sections. The graph returned five on 268 questions, fewer on 15, and nothing at all on 17, where the extractor found no concept on the question, so the traversal had no seed to start from. Those 17 are declines by construction rather than judgements about retrieved material.

A question where the traversal did something

Discussion 13108 asks how to get a frontend API and a backend API onto separate Swagger pages.

The question yields only four seed sections, so the fifth slot is the one the hop filled. It reaches Import APIRouter, which is what lets the answer rule an APIRouter out and reach for a mounted second application instead.
Path A retrieved Path B retrieved
Metadata and Docs URLs / Docs URLs First Steps / Interactive API docs
Path Parameters / Documentation Metadata and Docs URLs / Docs URLs
Declare Request Example Data / Pydantic and FastAPI examples Declare Request Example Data / Body with multiple examples
Tutorial User Guide Static Files / What is "Mounting"
First Steps / Interactive API docs Bigger Applications / Import APIRouter

The step that got it there is recorded on the answer, in the same form as every other claim in this repository. These two lines are copied out of data/answers.json and can be grepped for:

The traversal, as committed

q:13108 ASKS_ABOUT concept:sub_application -> DEFINES -> 1 section(s)

sec:tutorial/static-files#what-is-mounting REQUIRES concept:apirouter -> DEFINES -> sec:tutorial/bigger-applications#import-apirouter

The vector path declined this one. The graph path was scored correct, and its answer uses the section the hop pulled in: each application gets its own Swagger page, so the way to do this is to mount a second application rather than to add a router.

How often the hop actually reaches the answer

Rarely, and the design is why. Seeds are ranked first and the list is capped at five, so the one hop only reaches the final five when a question yields fewer than five seed sections. Across the 300 questions a hopped-in section survives into the context 70 times, and this example is one of only three where that happened and the graph was scored correct while the vector path was not.

The scores

Every generated answer was scored into one of three buckets against the maintainer's reply: correct, confidently wrong, meaning it asserts an answer that contradicts or omits the key fact with no hedge, and declined, meaning it said it could not answer from the material. Both answers to a question sat in the same batch, each scored on its own against that reply rather than as a comparison between the two. The scorer was not blind: every block is headed SCORE ID: q:13029::vector or SCORE ID: q:13029::graph, so the prompt named the path that produced the answer, and the vector answer came first in all 300 pairs. That is a defect in the setup and it is on the record in data/work/compare/score/in/. If it favoured anything it favoured the graph, whose three-answer lead is already not significant.

300 questions, both paths, 598 of 600 answers scored. One question came back without a verdict on either path, so both of its answers are counted as unscored rather than assigned to a bucket.
Path Correct Confidently wrong Declined
A, vector 25 5 269
B, graph 28 5 266

The headline metric this block exists to produce is the confidently wrong rate per path. It is 5 and 5. Identical.

On correctness the graph path is ahead by three, and because both paths answered the same 300 questions the comparison is paired, so the informative cases are the 27 questions where exactly one path got it right. The graph was alone in being correct on 15 of them and the vector path alone on 12. An exact McNemar test on that split gives p = 0.70.

On this corpus, graph retrieval did not measurably beat the vector baseline.

The traversal above is real and it does reach prerequisites lexical similarity cannot. It just did not change enough answers to be distinguishable from chance at this sample size, and reporting a three-answer lead as a result would be a worse mistake than reporting the null.

It is worth being specific about what "not enough" means here, because "underpowered" on its own tells a reader nothing they can act on. With 27 discordant pairs, the smallest split this test could have called significant at the 5% level is 20 against 7, which is a net edge of 13 questions out of 300. The observed edge was 3. So this design could only ever have detected a difference roughly four times larger than the one that showed up, and the null is much weaker evidence of no difference than the phrase suggests.

Why the experiment is underpowered, which is its own finding

Both paths declined about 90% of the questions, so the entire comparison rests on the 30 or so each one actually answered. That is not the retrieval, it is the question set.

282 of the 300 askers had already read the documentation and said so

That is what the template on FastAPI's Questions form asks people to confirm before posting, line by line: "I searched the FastAPI documentation, with the integrated search", and "I already read and followed all the tutorial in the docs and didn't find an answer." 282 of the 300 threads carry it.

Which makes the decline rate close to correct behaviour rather than a failure of retrieval. The demand signal is drawn, by construction, from the population for whom the documentation had already failed, and the corpus those questions are being answered from is the tutorial they had just finished reading. An assistant that answered most of them confidently from those 60 pages would be doing the thing this whole project is about.

I checked whether my own generation prompt had caused the abstaining, since it told the agent what was being measured, which is a good way to change the behaviour under test. Three batches rerun without that line declined 11 of 15 instead of 12 of 15, which is far too small a probe to detect a modest prompt effect and is reported here as the weak check it is.

This is a real cost of the decision in Part 3 to use public discussions as the demand signal. It works well for finding gaps, because the fourteen near misses in Part 4 all came from these same threads. It works badly as an answerability benchmark, and for a reason worth stating plainly: the sample is selected for being unanswerable from the documentation, so it cannot fairly measure how well anything answers from the documentation. A real team's search log would carry the answerable questions too, and this is the clearest place where free ground truth turned out to be worth exactly what was paid for it.

Prior work on exactly this comparison

Comparing retrieval strategies on whether a model abstains when it should is a well populated line of work, and the four benchmarks in Part 8 exist to measure exactly that. I am not citing a specific paper for the vector against graph version of it, because I did not find one I could check, and naming a reference I have not read would be a strange thing to do on this page. Treat the numbers above as one more data point, and a null one.

Part 6

Validation

Fourteen near misses is a small enough population to check all of them, so all of them were checked rather than a sample. Each one went to three independent readers, given the corpus, told to be sceptical, and told to go and search rather than judge from memory. A finding counts as valid only when no section of the corpus explains the concept and the answer on the thread supplies a fact the corpus does not contain.

Every near miss was checked, so the rate has no sampling error: it is the rate for this corpus and this run. The interval is reported anyway, as the uncertainty about the method rather than about the draw, and it is wide because the population is small.
Checked Held up Rate Wilson 95% Readers each Agreement
14 11 78.6% 0.52 to 0.92 3 Unanimous on all 14

Three did not hold up: path operation, fastapi.encoders and TokenData. They are still in the table in Part 4 and still in the explorer, marked as not holding up, because a list that quietly drops its own failures is not evidence of anything.

The path operation case is worth its own paragraph

All three readers agreed the corpus defines "path operation", and it does: First Steps gives it a titled step, then defines "Path" in one subsection and "Operation" in the next. No single heading-scoped chunk defines it, and the parent that ties the two halves together is two lines long.

The graph is section-level because retrieval is chunk-level. So the page teaches it and no retrievable piece of the page does, which is a real retrieval problem wearing the costume of a false positive. It is counted against the tool above, because that is the honest call under the rule as written.

A gap in FastAPI, or a gap in my index?

Everything above measures the findings against the 60 indexed pages. "These 60 pages never explain it" and "FastAPI never explains it" are different claims, and only the first one follows from a check run inside the corpus. A reader hears the second.

So the other 95 English documentation pages were fetched and all fourteen near misses put in front of three more readers, told to search only outside the corpus and to report either a definition with its file and line, or nothing.

The top right cell is the one that costs something. APIRoute is the second most asked about finding on the page, and FastAPI documents it properly in a how-to guide called Custom Request and APIRoute class, which is not in the 60. So does host, on the FastAPI CLI page, and virtual environments get a page of their own named after the concept. Those three are correct about the index and wrong about FastAPI, and a content team handed them would go and write something that already exists.

Eight of the fourteen are gaps. Three are my index pretending to be a gap.

This is the concrete version of the thing Part 7 states abstractly, and it cuts both ways. Measured as a claim about FastAPI, precision drops from 11 of 14 to 8 of 14. Measured as a claim about an assistant that indexes only the tutorial, all eleven stand, because from that assistant's side a page it was never given and a page nobody wrote are the same thing. Which number is right depends on which question you came with, and the honest thing is to publish both rather than pick the flattering one. It also names the cheapest improvement available to this project, which is not a better extractor: it is indexing the other 95 pages.

Why there is no pass or fail gate

A threshold at this population size would be decided by two or three items. The interval runs from 0.52 to 0.92, so a gate at 90% and a gate at 60% would disagree about the same fourteen findings. RefusalBench human validated 180 items and scored 93.1%, a rate whose Wilson 95% interval runs from 0.88 to 0.96, so a 90% gate could not separate work done by a funded team with expert annotators from failure.

So the number is reported with its interval and no gate is drawn. The build plan set one threshold, a floor at roughly 70% below which the right move is to stop and fix extraction rather than build on top of it. That is an instruction to whoever runs this, not a claim to a reader, and the observed rate clears it.

What this does and does not establish

It establishes that at the observed rate, a reported near miss is a genuine absence in this corpus, confirmed by an answer written outside it, and that three independent readers agree on every one of the fourteen.

It establishes nothing about the other 139 findings. The explorer ships 153, and the 78.6% covers only the 14 near misses. Every orphan concept and every retrieval collision carries validated: null in data/findings.json and is labelled "Not independently checked" on its card, which is the honest label and not a reassuring one.

It says nothing about recall. How many real gaps the pipeline never noticed is not measured here and could not be, because measuring it would need the hand labelling this design was built to avoid. Every guard in the pipeline also pushes the same way: the span validator, the scope rule and the definition sweep each drop findings rather than add them, so they buy precision with recall. Fourteen near misses across 60 pages is a floor, not an estimate.

Part 7

What this does not do

  • It does not measure recall, and cannot. Every number here is about precision: of the gaps reported, how many are real. How many real gaps were never noticed is unmeasured, and measuring it would need the hand labelling this whole design exists to avoid. Every fix in Part 6 traded recall for precision, so fourteen near misses over 60 pages is a floor and not an estimate.
  • The retrieval comparison is underpowered. Both paths declined about 90% of the questions, so the comparison rests on around thirty answers each. A null at p = 0.70 on that base is weak evidence of no difference, not evidence of no difference.
  • The demand signal is pre-filtered against the documentation. 282 of the 300 threads carry FastAPI's Questions template, in which the asker confirms they already read the tutorial and did not find the answer. That is excellent for finding gaps and useless for benchmarking answerability, because the sample is selected for being unanswerable from the docs. It is the price of the free ground truth in Part 3. A real team's search log would contain the answerable questions too.
  • Extraction quality bounds everything downstream. Every relation in the graph is a judgement a model made about one sentence, and three of the fourteen findings those judgements produced did not survive an independent check. That rate is measured and published, and whatever is still wrong in the extraction is still in these counts.
  • A corpus boundary looks exactly like a documentation gap. This one is now measured rather than argued. Three of the eleven near misses that held up are documented on a FastAPI page outside the 60, including the second most asked about finding on the page (Part 6). From a retriever's side a page you did not index and a page nobody wrote are the same thing, which is a real property of the problem, but it is not an excuse: the cheapest improvement available here is indexing the other 95 pages, and I have not done it.
  • One corpus, and a developer one. Everything here is FastAPI, so the method generalises in principle and is demonstrated in exactly one place. A consumer help centre is a different writing problem with no code samples to extract concepts from.
  • A sample, not an audit. 60 of 155 English pages and 300 of roughly 3,900 answered threads, and those 300 are not simply the most recent: a thread was skipped unless its accepted answer ran to 120 characters, because an answer of "+1" carries no fact that could be missing from the documentation. 67 were skipped to reach 300. The counts are counts within that slice.
  • No claim about a deployed assistant. This measures documentation. It does not measure escalations, deflection, or anything that happened to a real user.

Part 8

Prior work

None of the technique here is new, and it is worth being specific about who did what, because a portfolio project that quietly implies otherwise is worse than one that cites properly.

  • Sufficient Context, Google Research arXiv 2411.06037

    The 10% against 66% result, and the framing of sufficiency as the thing to measure rather than relevance. This is the method behind the near-miss finding. I did not come up with it and the finding type is an application of it. Read it.

  • Ragas open source

    Already builds a knowledge graph from a corpus, for the purpose of generating a test set. The graph-over-documentation idea is theirs and others', not mine. What is different here is the question asked of the graph and who the answer is addressed to.

  • Giskard open source

    Ships an out-of-scope question generator, which attacks a neighbouring problem: finding questions your assistant should refuse rather than articles that will make it answer wrongly.

  • BRINK, UAEval4RAG, RefusalBench, AbstentionBench benchmarks

    All cover measuring whether a model abstains when it should. The technique of evaluating abstention is well populated, which is exactly why this project does not claim it.

So what is the contribution

Routing, not method. Every piece of prior work above produces a number about a system, and hands it to whoever owns the system. Nobody who can fix a documentation gap can act on "your RAG pipeline scores 0.71 on sufficiency".

The output of this pipeline is addressed to the person who owns the content, in their units: this article, this missing fact, this many people asked, and here is the sentence in your own file that shows the article depends on something you never wrote. That is a work item. The benchmark score is not.

So the honest scope of this thing is a report, not a workflow. It manufactures a signal that did not exist, points at an article and names a missing fact, and orders the list by how many people asked. Then it stops. There is no export, no ticket, no owner and no done state, and a content lead who acted on it would be retyping findings into whatever system they actually work in.

That is the right place to have stopped for a demonstration, and it is the wrong place to stop for a product. If I were taking this further the next thing I would build is not more finding types. It is the export, because a finding that has to be retyped is a finding that competes with everything else in an inbox, and Part 4 already shows the yield is two urgent items per 60 pages. At that volume the tool has to cost almost nothing to act on, or the arithmetic never works.

Whether that repackaging is worth anything is a fair question, and this repository does not try to answer it with adoption numbers, because there are none and inventing a traction story would undercut everything else on this page.
