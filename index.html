Product teardown

Should My Agent Answer This?

When an AI assistant cannot find an answer in your documentation, it declines, somebody complains, and you learn something is missing. When it finds an article that is nearly right, it answers confidently and wrong, the metric records a success, and nobody ever tells you. This is a pipeline that goes looking for the second case on purpose, and this is the thinking behind it.

Roanuk Zaman · Product teardown of a tool I designed, directed and shipped

The short version

Summary

  • The dangerous failure is the one that looks like a success. Google Research measured it: models handed no supporting context answered incorrectly about 10% of the time, and models handed insufficient context about 66% of the time (Part 1). Every prioritisation signal a content team has surfaces the first case and none of them surface the second.
  • So the finding has to be manufactured, not collected. I built a knowledge graph over 60 pages of the FastAPI documentation where no edge exists without the verbatim sentence that justifies it, crossed it against 300 answered questions from the project's own GitHub Discussions, and looked for articles that depend on something the documentation never explains (Part 3). It produced 153 findings, 14 of them near misses (Part 4).
  • The first independent check failed, and that is the result worth reading. 9 of 33 near misses survived it. Under the build plan's own floor, which stopped the work before any of it was published (Part 6). Three of my own mistakes caused it. After fixing them, 11 of 14 survived, unanimously across three readers each. Both rounds are on this page, because a gap-finding tool is judged on its false positives and reporting only the second number would be the same failure this project is about.
  • Graph retrieval did not beat the vector baseline here. Both paths answered all 300 questions and both produced exactly 5 confidently wrong answers. The graph was alone in answering 15 correctly and the vector path alone in 12, which an exact paired test puts at p = 0.70 (Part 5). The experiment is also underpowered, for a reason worth knowing about the demand signal.

Part 1

The three outcomes

A company points an AI assistant at its documentation. Three things can happen.

It works. The answer is in the docs, retrieval finds it, the assistant answers correctly.

It finds nothing. No article is close, so the assistant declines. Annoying, but safe, and above all visible. The user complains, a ticket gets filed, a search with no results gets logged, and the content team learns that something is missing.

It finds something close but incomplete. An article looks relevant, gets retrieved, and is missing one fact the answer depends on. The assistant answers confidently and wrong. Nobody sees it, because the assistant resolved the request and the deflection metric counted it as a success. The article looks healthy forever.

The third case is not a rarer version of the second. It is substantially more dangerous, and that has been measured.

What Google Research measured

Across several models and datasets, answers were scored by whether the retrieved context was sufficient to answer the question. Given no supporting context at all, models answered incorrectly about 10% of the time, mostly by abstaining instead. Given insufficient context, they answered incorrectly about 66% of the time.

Partial information does not make a model more cautious. It makes it confident. Sufficient Context: A New Lens on Retrieval Augmented Generation Systems, arXiv 2411.06037.

That is the whole premise. An article that is 90% of an answer is worse than an article that is 0% of one, and it is the 90% article that no process in a content team is currently looking for.

Part 2

Why nobody sees the third one

Content teams do not prioritise at random. They prioritise from signals, and there are three of them.

Every routine prioritisation signal surfaces the safe failure and misses the dangerous one.
Signal Fires when the assistant declines Fires when the assistant is confidently wrong
Customer complaints Yes. The user did not get an answer and says so No. The user got an answer, believed it, and left
Escalations to a human Yes. The conversation gets handed over No. The assistant resolved it
Searches returning nothing Yes. That is exactly what the log records No. Retrieval returned five confident results
Deflection rate Counts against you, correctly Counts for you. The failure improves the metric

The failure mode that does the most damage is the one that makes the dashboard look better.

This is not an oversight in anyone's tooling. It is structural. Every one of those signals is a report of dissatisfaction, and the defining feature of a confident wrong answer is that nobody is dissatisfied at the moment it happens. The person who finds out is a customer, three days later, when the thing they were told does not work.

A signal that does not arrive cannot be collected. It has to be manufactured, before deployment, from the documentation itself. That is what the rest of this is.

Part 3

What I built

A pipeline that maps a documentation set as a knowledge graph, crosses it against questions real users actually asked, and produces a ranked list of the articles most likely to make an assistant answer confidently and wrong, naming the specific missing fact in each case.

The corpus, and why this one

60 pages of the FastAPI documentation, taken in the documentation's own reading order: all 51 tutorial pages, then the first 9 advanced pages, pinned to one commit.

The corpus is not the interesting decision. The demand signal is. The same repository runs a GitHub Discussions board with roughly 3,900 answered questions, so one source supplies the documentation, the questions people actually asked about it, and the correct answers, written by people who know. That last part is what removes the need to hand label anything.

The decision the whole project rests on

If a question was answered in a discussion and that answer is not in the documentation, then the gap is real and the missing content already exists in somebody's reply. Free ground truth, verifiable by anyone, and no annotation budget.

The cost is that it only sees gaps somebody bothered to ask about in public, which is a narrower set than a real team's search log.

The graph

Five node types and seven edge types. Deliberately small: this is a schema designed around one question, not a general ontology.

The three edges that matter are DEFINES, REQUIRES and ASKS_ABOUT. Everything else is scaffolding around them.
Edge From, to What it claims Count
DEFINES Section, Concept This section explains what the thing is or how to use it. A reader who knew nothing could learn it here 885
REQUIRES Section, Concept This section's instructions assume the reader already knows the thing. Following them without it would fail 1,611
MENTIONS Section, Concept Named in passing, neither explained nor depended upon 994
ASKS_ABOUT Question, Concept Answering this question requires understanding this concept 960
CONTAINS Doc, Section Structural 570
ANSWERED_BY Question, Answer GitHub marked this reply as the accepted answer 300
LINKS_TO Section, Doc An internal documentation link 50

The whole design turns on the distinction between DEFINES and REQUIRES. Every other graph over documentation I looked at models "this page is about that thing", which cannot express the finding, because being about a thing and teaching it are different claims.

The one rule everything else hangs off

No edge without evidence.

Every edge carries the verbatim source span that justifies it, plus the file path and the line range where that span lives. There are no exceptions, including for the structural edges where the span is obvious. It is the difference between "the graph says so" and "here is the sentence the graph read", and without it none of the findings are actionable, because nobody acts on a finding they cannot check.

It is also enforced rather than requested. A model asked for a verbatim span will sometimes paraphrase, so no span is believed. Each one is searched for in the file its edge claims it came from, and an edge whose span is not found there is dropped, not downgraded to low confidence. The permitted normalisations are Unicode form, whitespace, quote and dash shapes, and case, and that is the complete list.

What the enforcement actually caught

Of 3,509 documentation edges proposed across 79 batches, zero were dropped for an unfindable span. That is not a sign the check was unnecessary. It is a sign the instruction worked, and it is only knowable because the check exists. The check earned its place later, in Part 6, when it rejected two spans I was trying to add myself.

The same check on question spans dropped 13% on the first attempt, for a reason worth knowing: GitHub serves a discussion body twice, once as markdown and once as the plain text it renders to. The mapper read the plain text and the file on disk holds the markdown, so a span containing a backtick could not be found. Matching the two like with like recovered 124 of 125.

The three findings

Each one is a shape in the graph, and each one carries a proof path made of real edges you can follow to real lines in real files.

Type The shape The proof
F1 Near miss A Concept that Questions ask about, that a Section REQUIRES, and that no Section in the corpus DEFINES The asking span, the depending span, and the empty define set with the count of sections checked. Confirmed by the answer on the thread, quoted verbatim
F2 Orphan concept A Concept with REQUIRES or MENTIONS in-degree above zero, DEFINES in-degree of zero, and at least two sections leaning on it The in-edge list and the empty define set
F3 Retrieval collision Two Sections a single Question maps to with comparable concept overlap, where only one carries the answer The competing sections, their overlap scores, and each one's similarity to what the maintainer actually replied

Everything is ranked by demand, meaning the number of distinct discussion threads touching the concept. A gap nobody asks about is real and is not worth writing this quarter, and a tool that cannot tell the difference produces a backlog instead of a priority.

Fourteen is a small number, and that is the point

An earlier version of this section reported 57 near misses. It was wrong, and how it was wrong is Part 6. What is worth saying here is what the surviving fourteen have in common: almost all of them are demand of one. One person asked, once.

A gap nobody has asked about twice is still a gap, and it is not this quarter's work.

That is why demand is on every finding rather than being used as a filter. Two of the fourteen have real recurring demand, and a content lead with a week to spend should spend it on those two. The other twelve are a backlog, correctly identified and correctly ranked below them. A tool that could not tell those apart would hand over 153 equally urgent tasks, which is the same as handing over nothing.

Part 5

Vector against graph retrieval

Everything up to here builds and analyses a graph. That is knowledge graph work, not GraphRAG. GraphRAG means the graph drives retrieval that feeds generation, so both paths get built, both answer all 300 questions, and both get scored against the maintainer's own reply.

Path How it retrieves
A, vector baseline Every Section and every Question embedded with sentence-transformers all-MiniLM-L6-v2, pinned, local, on CPU. Top five sections by cosine similarity
B, graph retrieval Seed on the Sections that DEFINE the concepts the question asks about. Then one hop: for each seed, also retrieve the Sections that DEFINE anything that seed REQUIRES. Capped at five to match

The traversal is the point. Path B can pull in the prerequisite a section depends on. Path A cannot, because a prerequisite is not lexically similar to the question that needs it. Across the 300 questions the two paths never once returned the same five sections, and in 282 of them the graph reached at least one section the vector path did not.

A question where the traversal did something

Discussion 14334 asks why jsonable_encoder is not respecting exclude on a Field.

The vector path retrieves five things about excluding and about OpenAPI. The graph path retrieves the section on the actual function, and then the section that defines Field, which is what the question turns on.

Custom Response / JSON Responses Body Fields / Import Field
Path Operation Advanced Configuration / Custom OpenAPI content type Body Fields / Declare model attributes

sec:advanced/response-directly#using-the-jsonable-encoder-in-a-response REQUIRES concept:basemodel → DEFINES → sec:tutorial/body#create-your-data-model

The scores

Every generated answer was scored into one of three buckets against the maintainer's reply: correct, confidently wrong, meaning it asserts an answer that contradicts or omits the key fact with no hedge, and declined, meaning it said it could not answer from the material. The scorer saw the two answers side by side without being told which retrieval path produced which.

300 questions, both paths, 599 of 600 answers scored. One answer came back without a verdict and is counted as unscored rather than assigned to a bucket.
Path Correct Confidently wrong Declined
A, vector 25 5 269
B, graph 28 5 266

The headline metric this block exists to produce is the confidently wrong rate per path. It is 5 and 5. Identical.

On correctness the graph path is ahead by three, and because both paths answered the same 300 questions the comparison is paired, so the informative cases are the 32 where the two verdicts differ. The graph was alone in being correct on 15 of them and the vector path alone on 12. An exact McNemar test on that split gives p = 0.70.

On this corpus, graph retrieval did not measurably beat the vector baseline.

The traversal above is real and it does reach prerequisites lexical similarity cannot. It just did not change enough answers to be distinguishable from chance at this sample size, and reporting a three-answer lead as a result would be a worse mistake than reporting the null.

Why the experiment is underpowered, which is its own finding

Both paths declined about 90% of the questions, so the entire comparison rests on the 30 or so each one actually answered. That is not the retrieval, it is the question set.

282 of the 300 threads carry FastAPI's bug-report template

The Questions category on that repository is used mostly for bug reports, version-specific breakage and feature discussion, not for "how do I". A documentation assistant genuinely cannot answer "background tasks run asynchronously only on Windows", and declining is the correct behaviour, not a failure of retrieval.

I checked whether my own generation prompt had caused the abstaining, since it told the agent what was being measured, which is a good way to change the behaviour under test. Three batches rerun without that line declined 11 of 15 instead of 12 of 15. The prompt was not the cause. The corpus is.

This is a real cost of the decision in Part 3 to use public discussions as the demand signal. It works well for finding gaps, because the fourteen near misses in Part 4 all came from these same threads. It works badly as an answerability benchmark, because most of these questions were never answerable from documentation by anyone. A real team's search log would not have that problem, and this is the clearest place where free ground truth turned out to be worth exactly what was paid for it.

Prior work on exactly this comparison

A vector against graph retrieval comparison on abstention behaviour has been published before. This reproduces the comparison on a new corpus and does not claim the idea. Treat the numbers above as one more data point, and a null one.

Part 6

Validation

The build plan set one hard rule for this step: if observed validity comes in under roughly 70%, stop and fix extraction before building anything on top, and report the real number whatever it is. It came in under, so that is what happened, and this is the number.

Both rounds checked every near-miss finding rather than a sample, because in each case the population was smaller than the target of 50. The rate therefore has no sampling error, and the interval describes the method rather than the draw.
Round Checked Held up Rate Wilson 95% Readers each
1, as first built 33 9 27.3% 0.15 to 0.44 1
2, after the fixes 14 11 78.6% 0.52 to 0.92 3, unanimous on all 14

What round one caught

Every one of the 24 rejections was my mistake, in one of three kinds.

  • Seven were not documentation gaps at all. The concept space contained bare Python keywords and builtins, so the tool was reporting that the FastAPI documentation never explains str. True, and useless, because explaining str is Python's job. The fix is a scope rule: a gap only counts if closing it is this documentation set's responsibility. The language half of that rule is generated from Python's own keyword list and builtins rather than hand-written, so it cannot be quietly tuned to make a number look better.
  • Sixteen were definitions my extractor had missed. The corpus does explain the thing and the graph did not know. A second sweep, rewritten around the specific places the checker had found them, recovered 32 more definitions on top of the 74 the first sweep found.
  • One was a name local to a code sample, which the tutorial defines in the very sample it is teaching.

The fix that had to be undone

While fixing those I added a rule that excluded any concept the corpus only ever shows inside code samples, reasoning that such a thing is an example variable rather than a documented concept. It cut the near misses to six and the numbers looked much better.

It had also deleted four findings that round one had explicitly confirmed as real: jwt.decode, session.exec, exc.errors() and Literal. Appearing only inside code samples is not evidence that something is an example variable. It is exactly what a genuine gap in third-party API surface looks like, which is the whole category this tool exists to find.

The rule was removed. It survives as a label on each card rather than a filter, so a reader can see how the corpus treats a concept without the tool having silently decided for them.

Round one was itself wrong four times

Six findings survived the fixes that round one had rejected. Rather than assume either side was right, three independent readers were given round one's own file-and-line evidence and asked to confirm or refute it. They found round one wrong on four of the six. The line it cited as defining "port" defines origin, and port is only named inside that definition. The line it cited for "host" is not prose at all, it is inside a fenced code block.

On the two where the readers agreed a definition existed, the proposed spans went through the same validator as every other edge and both were rejected, because each one straddles a section boundary. No edge was added on a span that could not be checked, and the invariant held under pressure from me rather than from a model.

The path operation case is worth its own paragraph

Three readers agreed the corpus defines "path operation", and it does: First Steps gives it a titled step, then defines "Path" in one subsection and "Operation" in the next. No single heading-scoped chunk defines it, and the parent that ties the two halves together is two lines long.

The graph is section-level because retrieval is chunk-level. So the page teaches it and no retrievable piece of the page does, which is a real retrieval problem wearing the costume of a false positive. It is counted as a false positive above, because that is the honest call under the rule as written.

Why there is no pass or fail gate

A threshold at this population size would be decided by two or three items. Round two's interval runs from 0.52 to 0.92, so a gate at 90% and a gate at 60% would disagree about the same fourteen findings. RefusalBench human validated 180 items and scored 93.1%, which means a 90% gate would fail work done by a funded team with expert annotators.

So the number is reported with its interval and no gate is drawn. The one threshold that exists is the 70% floor, and it is an instruction to me rather than a claim to a reader. It fired, and it did its job.

What this does and does not establish

It establishes that at the observed rate, a near miss in the final set is a genuine absence in this corpus, confirmed by an answer written outside it, and that three independent readers agree on every one of the fourteen.

It says nothing about recall. How many real gaps the pipeline never noticed is not measured here and could not be, because measuring it would need the hand labelling this design was built to avoid. Worse, the fixes in this section were all precision fixes, and every one of them can only have lowered recall. Fourteen near misses across 60 pages is a floor, not an estimate.

Part 7

What this does not do

  • It does not measure recall, and cannot. Every number here is about precision: of the gaps reported, how many are real. How many real gaps were never noticed is unmeasured, and measuring it would need the hand labelling this whole design exists to avoid. Every fix in Part 6 traded recall for precision, so fourteen near misses over 60 pages is a floor and not an estimate.
  • The retrieval comparison is underpowered. Both paths declined about 90% of the questions, so the comparison rests on around thirty answers each. A null at p = 0.70 on that base is weak evidence of no difference, not evidence of no difference.
  • The demand signal is a bug tracker. 282 of the 300 threads carry FastAPI's bug-report template. That is fine for finding gaps and poor for benchmarking answerability, and it is the price of the free ground truth in Part 3. A real team's search log would be a different and better input.
  • Extraction quality bounds everything downstream. This is not a hedge, it is the measured behaviour: one bad line in one prompt sent the first validation round to 27%, and it took an independent check plus two sweeps to find why. Whatever is still wrong in the extraction is still in these counts.
  • A corpus boundary looks exactly like a documentation gap. Several early findings were concepts FastAPI documents on pages outside the 60. The scope rule now removes the ones that are Python's job, and the general problem does not go away: from a retriever's side, a page you did not index and a page nobody wrote are the same thing. That is a genuine property of the problem rather than a flaw in the tool, and it means the output has to be read against the index a real assistant would be given.
  • One corpus, and a developer one. Everything here is FastAPI, so the method generalises in principle and is demonstrated in exactly one place. A consumer help centre is a different writing problem with no code samples to extract concepts from.
  • A sample, not an audit. 60 of 155 English pages and 300 of roughly 3,900 answered threads. The counts are counts within that slice.
  • No claim about a deployed assistant. This measures documentation. It does not measure escalations, deflection, or anything that happened to a real user.

Part 8

Prior work

None of the technique here is new, and it is worth being specific about who did what, because a portfolio project that quietly implies otherwise is worse than one that cites properly.

  • Sufficient Context, Google Research arXiv 2411.06037

    The 10% against 66% result, and the framing of sufficiency as the thing to measure rather than relevance. This is the method behind the near-miss finding. I did not come up with it and the finding type is an application of it. Read it.

  • Ragas open source

    Already builds a knowledge graph from a corpus, for the purpose of generating a test set. The graph-over-documentation idea is theirs and others', not mine. What is different here is the question asked of the graph and who the answer is addressed to.

  • Giskard open source

    Ships an out-of-scope question generator, which attacks a neighbouring problem: finding questions your assistant should refuse rather than articles that will make it answer wrongly.

  • BRINK, UAEval4RAG, RefusalBench, AbstentionBench benchmarks

    All cover measuring whether a model abstains when it should. The technique of evaluating abstention is well populated, which is exactly why this project does not claim it.

So what is the contribution

Routing, not method. Every piece of prior work above produces a number about a system, and hands it to whoever owns the system. Nobody who can fix a documentation gap can act on "your RAG pipeline scores 0.71 on sufficiency".

The output of this pipeline is addressed to the person who owns the content, in their units: this article, this missing fact, this many people asked, and here is the sentence in your own file that shows the article depends on something you never wrote. That is a work item. The benchmark score is not.

Whether that repackaging is worth anything is a fair question, and this repository does not try to answer it with adoption numbers, because there are none and inventing a traction story would undercut everything else on this page.
